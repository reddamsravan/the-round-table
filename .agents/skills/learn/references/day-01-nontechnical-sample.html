Day 1: First Principles Thinking

Curriculum: Critical Thinking Level: beginner Daily Target: 30 min Primary Goal: Make clear choices

Learning Objectives

  • Tell apart basic reasoning and copycat thinking.
  • Break hard problems down to bare facts.
  • Build fresh answers upward from bedrock truth.
Core Mental Model

The Big Idea: Boil any problem down to things you know are true. Build up from there.

Most people copy what others do. A first principles thinker asks what nature and logic strictly permit.

Predict and Inquire

Active Hypothesis Check

In 2010, car battery packs cost six hundred dollars for each unit of power. Most experts said costs could never drop fast. They said past supply contracts set that price.

Your Question: How would a first principles thinker test this claim?

Check Your Prediction and See the Analysis

Outcome: Look up the raw material costs on the open market.

Why: A battery holds carbon, nickel, aluminum, and steel. Buying those raw metals directly costs eighty dollars. The rest is factory work and assembly choices, not physical law.

Deep Dive and Applied Scenarios

Most thinking relies on analogy. People look at what worked before and make tiny tweaks. That feels safe, but it stops big breakthroughs.

First principles uses three clear steps:

  1. Spot Assumptions: Name the common rule or belief.
  2. Strip to Facts: Dig down until you find facts you cannot dispute.
  3. Build Up: Form a new plan straight from those bare facts.

Two Ways of Thinking Compared

Area Copying Others First Principles
Starting Point Past habits and industry norms Basic facts, math, and physical law
Speed Quick with low mental strain Slow and needs deep focus
Result Small, slow gains Original ideas and huge leaps

Case Study 1: Launching Rockets

Rocket prices looked fixed by old habits. An engineer asked what raw metal was in a rocket. Aluminum and titanium cost only two percent of the total price. Better tool choices cut launch costs by ninety percent.

Case Study 2: Designing Food Menus

A new cook often copies menus from across the street. A smart cook asks what fresh crops cost this week. They build simple dishes that waste zero food and taste great.

Common Misconceptions and Traps

Pitfall 1: Questioning Every Small Choice

The Trap: Trying to rethink every small habit from scratch.

The Reality: Deep thinking takes big mental energy. Use habits for daily chores like buying milk. Save deep reasoning for big goals and key hurdles.

Pitfall 2: Treating Habits as Hard Laws

The Trap: Believing "This is how we always do it" means it must stay that way.

The Reality: Most work rules reflect old social habits. Test whether a rule is a physical law or just an old custom.

Comprehension Self-Check

Answer each question in your own words before revealing the answer.

Question 1: What is the main risk of copying other people?

Copying traps you in old forms. You miss great answers when the real world changes.

Question 2: How do you know you reached a bedrock fact?

You cannot simplify the fact any further. It stands on direct observation, math, or physical law.

Question 3: When should you skip deep reasoning?

Skip it on low-risk tasks where speed matters far more than fresh insight.

Practical Tasks

Tier 1: Analyze and Critique 5 - 10 min

Test an Old Habit

Pick one habit from your job or daily routine. Write down two silent claims behind it. Label each claim as a physical fact or a social habit.

Tier 2: Core Application 15 - 30 min

Bedrock Cost Teardown

Doubt Your Model

Take your Tier 2 draft plan. Ask: "What if one of my three facts is false?" Write down what breaks and how to fix it.
